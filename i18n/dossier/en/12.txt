10. OLIGOPOLY, COURNOT, BERTRAND AND STACKELBERG — L10

10.1 Choosing the right game
Oligopoly: a small number of firms whose decisions directly affect each other.
Cournot: quantities chosen simultaneously; homogeneous product in the base case.
Bertrand: prices chosen simultaneously; state whether homogeneous or differentiated.
Stackelberg: a leader chooses first, a follower observes and reacts.
Nash: no firm gains by deviating UNILATERALLY, the other strategies
being fixed. The intersection of best responses is a Nash equilibrium.
A best response is a choice conditional on the rival, not a promise
to hold that rival constant after every change to the model.

10.2 Linear Cournot with two firms
p=a-b(q1+q2), costs C_i=F_i+c_i*q_i.
pi1=[a-b(q1+q2)-c1]*q1-F1.
partial pi1/partial q1=a-2bq1-bq2-c1=0.
BR1(q2)=max(0,(a-c1-bq2)/(2b)), BR2 likewise.
If both are active:
q1=(a-2c1+c2)/(3b), q2=(a-2c2+c1)/(3b),
Q=(2a-c1-c2)/(3b), p=(a+c1+c2)/3.
If a quantity is negative, solve the corner where that firm does not produce.
Then compute pi_i=(p-c_i)q_i-F_i.
Inside this linear model, p-c_i=bq_i ->variable profit=bq_i^2.
Differences in unavoidable FC do not change these quantities; they change
profits and can change entry/exit decisions.

L10 example a70,b1,c1=c2=10: BR_i=30-q_j/2;
q1=q2=20,Q40,p30,pi_i400 without FC.
Joint monopoly Q30,p40; perfect competition Q60,p10.
With MC Blue0, Red10: qBlue80/3≈26.667, qRed50/3≈16.667,
p80/3≈26.667. The efficient firm takes more of the market; imposing equal
shares can make production more expensive. Large shares do not on their own
prove inefficiency.

10.3 Cournot with n identical firms
p=a-bQ, MCc: q_i=(a-c)/[b(n+1)],
Q=n(a-c)/[b(n+1)], p=(a+n*c)/(n+1).
Lerner=(p-c)/p=-(market share_i)/epsilon_market for this model,
so under symmetry -1/(n*epsilon_market).
As n grows: p tends to c, Q to the competitive quantity.
A merger reducing n, without cost synergy: p rises; the non-merged firms
often benefit from this reduced competition in this model.
Do not generalise this result to every merger with changing costs/products.

10.4 Drawing Cournot reactions
Axes q1 horizontal/q2 vertical OR the reverse, but keep the same convention.
If q1 is horizontal: BR2 is drawn directly as q2=f(q1), and BR1 must be
rewritten q2=(a-c1)/b-2q1 in the interior zone.
Both slope downward; intersection = Nash quantities.
Cost c1 rises: BR1 changes, BR2 as a function of q1 does not change;
the rival responds to the new equilibrium. A tax on both firms: shifts
BOTH reactions towards lower output. This is E2022 Q3e.

10.5 Stackelberg in quantities
First solve BR_follower(qL), substitute it into the leader's profit,
then differentiate with respect to qL. Do not treat qF as constant here.
Symmetric case p=a-bQ, MCc, no capacity constraints:
qL=(a-c)/(2b), qF=(a-c)/(4b), Q=3(a-c)/(4b), p=(a+3c)/4.
The leader produces more and earns more than under Cournot; the follower produces less.
Total Q rises, p falls. The result depends on the model's assumptions.
L10 a70,c10: qL30,qF15,Q45,p25; profits 450 and 225.
The Stackelberg equilibrium lies on the follower's reaction, usually not
on the leader's Cournot reaction, which holds the rival's quantity fixed.

10.6 Homogeneous Bertrand
Perfectly substitutable products, same constant MC, sufficient capacity,
customers buying from the cheapest: with two or more firms, p=MC.
If p>MC, undercutting slightly can capture the market; p<MC loses
money on each unit. Lerner0 for p=MC>0.
Limited capacity, differentiation, search or repetition can change
the result; the number of firms alone does not determine the markup.
Different costs: the lowest-cost firm can keep a markup by
limiting its price by the cost of the most efficient competitor.
E2024 MC5,7,8 and p_D10-Q: limit price around 7, often written 7-epsilon.
The monopoly price without a competitor would be 7.5, so the rival's constraint
is relevant. With continuous prices, existence/tie-breaking need precision:
price exactly 7 if there is a suitable rule allocating to the efficient producer;
with equal sharing at a tie, the approximation of slight undercutting is
more accurate than claiming a pure equilibrium at 7.

10.7 Differentiated Bertrand
Demand q_i=A-B*p_i+D*p_j, B>0,D>=0, MCc_i.
pi_i=(p_i-c_i)*(A-Bp_i+Dp_j)-F_i.
FOC: A-2Bp_i+Dp_j+B*c_i=0.
BR_i(p_j)=(A+B*c_i+D*p_j)/(2B).
Upward-sloping reactions for substitutes: prices are strategic complements.
Under symmetry: p=(A+B*c)/(2B-D), if domain/concavity are admissible.
Quantity via the DEMAND; profit=(p-c)q-F.
L10 A85,B1,D0.5,c11 ->p64,q53,pi2809 without FC.
E2025 A10,B2,D1: p=(10+2c)/3.
c2 ->p14/3; c1 ->p4. Cost falls by 1 ->price falls by 2/3.
Do not apply competitive pass-through to this market structure.

10.8 Price leadership
Substitute the follower's PRICE reaction into the leader's demand,
before maximising its profit. Not the Stackelberg quantity formula.
E2024 Q4c/d: q1=6-2p1+p2, q2=6-2p2+p1; no cost is specified.
Under the explicit assumption MC=FC=0: simultaneous p1=p2=2, q1=q2=4, pi_i8.
If firm2 leads: p1=(6+p2)/4; demand2=7.5-1.75p2;
p2=15/7≈2.143, p1=57/28≈2.036; pi2=225/28≈8.036,
pi1=3249/392≈8.288. BOTH gain here; the follower gains more.
The "first-mover advantage" in quantities is not a universal law
for all games, especially when prices are complements.
