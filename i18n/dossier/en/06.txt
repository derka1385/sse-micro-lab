======================================================================
4. CONSUMER, PREFERENCES, BUDGET AND DEMAND — L3–L4
======================================================================

4.1 Preferences and utility
Completeness: any two bundles can be ranked, indifference included.
Transitivity: if A is preferred to B and B to C, A is preferred to C.
Non-satiation / more-is-better: more of desirable goods is preferred.
Ordinal utility: U ranks bundles; a utility of 100 does not mean
twice as much happiness as a utility of 50. A strictly increasing
transformation represents the same preferences.
Example L3: U(E,F)=sqrt(EF), with E entertainment, F food.
U=EF gives the same ranking for positive E,F; the numerical levels
and the derivatives change, not the optimal choices.

MU_x = partial derivative of U with respect to x: marginal utility of good x.
Diminishing marginal utility: one more unit adds less and less
utility, for a given quantity of the other good. Do not confuse this
property with the ordinal nature of utility.

Indifference curve: bundles giving the same U. Under usual preferences,
it is downward sloping, convex to the origin, does not cross another curve
of the same consumer; curves further north-east are preferred.
Two desirable goods: a positive slope is incompatible with more-is-better.
If tea is horizontal and coffee vertical, horizontal indifference curves: only
coffee matters; tea gives no marginal utility (L3 p.10).

4.2 MRS and budget: never swap the axes without adapting the ratios
With x horizontal and y vertical, along an indifference curve:
0=MU_x*dx+MU_y*dy ->dy/dx=-MU_x/MU_y.
MRS_xy=MU_x/MU_y, absolute value of that slope: amount of y the
consumer is willing to give up for one extra unit of x.
In L3, F horizontal and E vertical: slope=-MU_F/MU_E.

Budget p_x*x+p_y*y <= I; equality at the optimum if non-satiation and positive prices.
y=I/p_y-(p_x/p_y)*x; intercepts x=I/p_x and y=I/p_y.
Income rises: parallel outward shift, prices unchanged.
Price of x rises: y-intercept unchanged, x-intercept falls, budget steeper.
A price change reduces purchasing power; it does not necessarily change
nominal income I.

4.3 Interior optimum
Max U(x,y) subject to the budget. At an interior tangency:
MU_x/MU_y=p_x/p_y, i.e. MU_x/p_x=MU_y/p_y.
Intuition: the last euro brings the same marginal utility in both
uses. Otherwise moving a small amount of spending to the better use raises U.
Use this condition TOGETHER WITH the budget, not instead of it.
It assumes an interior solution and sufficiently
regular preferences; compare corners and kinks when needed.

Computational complement, Cobb-Douglas U=x^alpha*y^beta, alpha,beta>0:
x*=alpha/(alpha+beta)*I/p_x; y*=beta/(alpha+beta)*I/p_y.
Justification: MRS=(alpha/beta)*(y/x)=p_x/p_y, then the budget.
For U=xy: half of income on each good, not half of the number of units.

4.4 Perfect substitutes and complements
Perfect substitutes U=ax+by: MRS=a/b constant; compare a/p_x with b/p_y.
All on x if a/p_x>b/p_y; all on y if the reverse; any mix on the
budget is optimal if equal. A unique tangency is not required.
Example L4 p.10: 2 macchiatos equivalent to 1 cappuccino, same prices:
buy only cappuccinos, more utility per euro.
Perfect complements U=min(x/a,y/b): choose at the kink x/a=y/b,
because unmatched units add nothing. Example left/right shoes.
For a 1:1 pair: x=y=I/(p_x+p_y).

4.5 Demand and income
Recompute the optimum for each price of x, keeping I and the other prices
constant; the pairs (p_x,x*) trace the individual demand.
Engel curve: optimal quantity as a function of INCOME, prices constant.
Normal good: quantity rises with I; inferior: falls with I.
The same good can be normal at low income and inferior at high income
(L4 example of hostel nights).
Luxury good: income elasticity >1; normal necessity: between 0 and 1.
These labels do not rank the intrinsic quality of the good.

4.6 Substitution, income and compensation
A price rise makes x relatively more expensive: substitution towards y.
It also reduces purchasing power: income effect.
L4 p.11–13: tax on petrol, then a lump-sum transfer just enough to
reach the OLD indifference curve at the NEW prices. Utility can be
maintained while petrol consumption falls through substitution.
This transfer does not remove the new relative price.
E2022 Q5: transfer allowing the OLD BUNDLE to be bought after removing
a food subsidy. The old bundle remains affordable; with an
old interior solution and a strictly convex indifference curve,
re-choosing on the new budget usually gives higher utility.
Old bundle affordable and old utility just reached are two
distinct compensation criteria. The general guarantee is "no worse
off"; strict improvement needs tangency/corner conditions.

4.7 Market demand
QD(p)=sum_i max(0,q_i(p)). HORIZONTAL summation at a given price.
L4 p.19: q_H=10-p, q_L=5-p.
QD=0 for p>=10; QD=10-p for 5<p<10; QD=15-2p for 0<=p<=5.
The aggregate inverse demand has a kink; check the segment of any optimum.

4.8 Behaviour, social preferences and limits
L3: utility can include altruism, status, respect, pride and spite.
Maximising utility is therefore not the same as maximising one's own consumption.
Ultimatum game: a positive money offer may be rejected for fairness.
Lack of self-control: immediate gratification against future interest.
Heuristics: simple rules when optimisation is hard.
Loss aversion: losses can weigh more than comparable gains.
Framing: how a choice is presented affects the decision.
Overconfidence: excessive confidence in one's abilities/information.
Social preferences in A3: convert money payoffs into UTILITIES
before looking for best responses.
