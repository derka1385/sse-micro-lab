2. COMMON LOGIC AND MATHEMATICAL TOOLS
======================================================================

2.1 The skeleton of almost every problem
1) Who decides? Consumer, firm, bank, authority, bidder.
2) What objective? Utility, profit, minimum cost or SOCIAL surplus.
3) Which variable do they choose? Quantity, price, effort, inputs, contract.
4) What is given? Prices, income, rival, technology, information.
5) Which constraints? Budget, production, capacity, non-negativity,
   participation, incentive, no resale, follower's reaction.
6) Write the objective function and the consistency relations.
7) Solve; check domain, corners, optimum and participation.
8) Go back to the prices, quantities, profits and surplus asked for.
9) Explain why a small deviation does not improve the objective.

"Marginal" means the effect of one extra unit, or the derivative
in a continuous model. "Average" means total divided by quantity.
A marginal decision compares extra benefit and extra
cost. An unavoidable fixed cost does not enter this comparison,
but it counts in total profit and in the decision to stay in the long run.

2.2 Derivatives and optimality conditions
y=f(x): f'(x)=dy/dx, local slope. For several variables, use
a partial derivative holding the others constant.
d(k)/dx=0; d(ax)/dx=a; d(x^n)/dx=n*x^(n-1).
d[f(x)g(x)]/dx=f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x), product rule.
For p(Q)Q, MR=p(Q)+Q*p'(Q), not just p'(Q)Q.
For a concave function, the FOC f'(x)=0 gives an interior maximum.
Check f''(x)<0 or compare values at the boundaries if needed.
A FOC alone guarantees neither a maximum, nor a feasible quantity, nor production.

For an expected sum: E[X]=sum(probability of the state * outcome).
Compute the NET outcome in each state before weighting.
A penalty applied with probability d has an expected cost d*F.

2.3 Areas and units
Rectangle = base*height; triangle = base*height/2.
Surplus = area between marginal willingness to pay and the relevant cost/price.
General form: area = integral of the vertical difference as a function of Q.
Triangles assume linear curves over the interval considered.
Price in money/unit * quantity in units = money.
If Q is in THOUSANDS of tonnes and p in dollars/tonne, the area is in
THOUSANDS of dollars. This point changes the result by a factor of 1000.
An elasticity has no unit; a slope usually has one.

2.4 Rewrite before calculating
Direct demand Q=a-bp -> inverse demand p=a/b-Q/b.
Direct supply Q=-c+dp -> inverse supply p=c/d+Q/d.
On a market graph: Q horizontal, p vertical; draw the inverse
forms. To add up private demands: add the quantities
at the same price, not the prices. For a public good: add the marginal
benefits at the same Q, i.e. vertically.

3. SUPPLY, DEMAND, EQUILIBRIUM AND SHOCKS — L2
======================================================================

Demand: the quantity buyers want to buy at each price.
Supply: the quantity sellers want to sell at each price.
Assumptions of the simple competitive model: many agents, homogeneous
products, price takers; no significant strategic interaction.
Define the market by product, geographic area and period.

Example L2 p.7/16/20: QD=10-p, QS=-2+p.
Equilibrium: QD=QS ->10-p=-2+p ->p*=6, Q*=4.
At p=4: QD=6, QS=2, excess demand of 4, upward pressure.
At p=8: QD=2, QS=6, excess supply of 4, downward pressure.
A negative quantity predicted outside the domain usually means quantity 0.

CHANGE IN THE GOOD'S OWN PRICE -> movement ALONG its curve.
CHANGE IN A DETERMINANT OTHER THAN THAT PRICE -> shift of the curve.
Demand QD=f(p,T,p_r,I): tastes/temperature T, other price p_r, income I.
Normal good: income rises -> demand shifts right.
Inferior good: income rises -> demand shifts left.
Substitutes: price of the other good rises -> demand shifts right.
Complements: price of the other good rises -> demand shifts left.
Input cost rises -> supply shifts left/up.
Better technology/entry of producers -> supply shifts right/down.

Other things equal, for usual curves:
D right: p rises, Q rises. D left: p falls, Q falls.
S right: p falls, Q rises. S left: p rises, Q falls.
D right AND S left: p rises, effect on Q ambiguous.
D right AND S right: Q rises, effect on p ambiguous.
D left AND S left: Q falls, effect on p ambiguous.
D left AND S right: p falls, effect on Q ambiguous.
Do not conclude on an ambiguous variable without the relative size of the shocks.

Sensitivity: for a given demand shock, a steep supply implies
a large price change and a small quantity change.
A flatter supply absorbs more of the shock through quantity.
L2 applications: wheat harvests, tea/coffee, rice, palladium.
Explain with "at a given price, more/less would be supplied/demanded",
and only then look for the new equilibrium.

4. CONSUMER, PREFERENCES, BUDGET AND DEMAND — L3–L4
======================================================================

4.1 Preferences and utility
Completeness: any two bundles can be ranked, indifference included.
Transitivity: if A is preferred to B and B to C, A is preferred to C.
Non-satiation / more-is-better: more of desirable goods is preferred.
Ordinal utility: U ranks bundles; a utility of 100 does not mean
twice as much happiness as a utility of 50. A strictly increasing
transformation represents the same preferences.
Example L3: U(E,F)=sqrt(EF), with E entertainment, F food.
U=EF gives the same ranking for positive E,F; the numerical levels
and the derivatives change, not the optimal choices.

MU_x = partial derivative of U with respect to x: marginal utility of good x.
Diminishing marginal utility: one more unit adds less and less
utility, for a given quantity of the other good. Do not confuse this
property with the ordinal nature of utility.

Indifference curve: bundles giving the same U. Under usual preferences,
it is downward sloping, convex to the origin, does not cross another curve
of the same consumer; curves further north-east are preferred.
Two desirable goods: a positive slope is incompatible with more-is-better.
If tea is horizontal and coffee vertical, horizontal indifference curves: only
coffee matters; tea gives no marginal utility (L3 p.10).

4.2 MRS and budget: never swap the axes without adapting the ratios
With x horizontal and y vertical, along an indifference curve:
0=MU_x*dx+MU_y*dy ->dy/dx=-MU_x/MU_y.
MRS_xy=MU_x/MU_y, absolute value of that slope: amount of y the
consumer is willing to give up for one extra unit of x.
In L3, F horizontal and E vertical: slope=-MU_F/MU_E.

Budget p_x*x+p_y*y <= I; equality at the optimum if non-satiation and positive prices.
y=I/p_y-(p_x/p_y)*x; intercepts x=I/p_x and y=I/p_y.
Income rises: parallel outward shift, prices unchanged.
Price of x rises: y-intercept unchanged, x-intercept falls, budget steeper.
A price change reduces purchasing power; it does not necessarily change
nominal income I.

4.3 Interior optimum
Max U(x,y) subject to the budget. At an interior tangency:
MU_x/MU_y=p_x/p_y, i.e. MU_x/p_x=MU_y/p_y.
Intuition: the last euro brings the same marginal utility in both
uses. Otherwise moving a small amount of spending to the better use raises U.
Use this condition TOGETHER WITH the budget, not instead of it.
It assumes an interior solution and sufficiently
regular preferences; compare corners and kinks when needed.

Computational complement, Cobb-Douglas U=x^alpha*y^beta, alpha,beta>0:
x*=alpha/(alpha+beta)*I/p_x; y*=beta/(alpha+beta)*I/p_y.
Justification: MRS=(alpha/beta)*(y/x)=p_x/p_y, then the budget.
For U=xy: half of income on each good, not half of the number of units.

4.4 Perfect substitutes and complements
Perfect substitutes U=ax+by: MRS=a/b constant; compare a/p_x with b/p_y.
All on x if a/p_x>b/p_y; all on y if the reverse; any mix on the
budget is optimal if equal. A unique tangency is not required.
Example L4 p.10: 2 macchiatos equivalent to 1 cappuccino, same prices:
buy only cappuccinos, more utility per euro.
Perfect complements U=min(x/a,y/b): choose at the kink x/a=y/b,
because unmatched units add nothing. Example left/right shoes.
For a 1:1 pair: x=y=I/(p_x+p_y).

4.5 Demand and income
Recompute the optimum for each price of x, keeping I and the other prices
constant; the pairs (p_x,x*) trace the individual demand.
Engel curve: optimal quantity as a function of INCOME, prices constant.
Normal good: quantity rises with I; inferior: falls with I.
The same good can be normal at low income and inferior at high income
(L4 example of hostel nights).
Luxury good: income elasticity >1; normal necessity: between 0 and 1.
These labels do not rank the intrinsic quality of the good.

4.6 Substitution, income and compensation
A price rise makes x relatively more expensive: substitution towards y.
It also reduces purchasing power: income effect.
L4 p.11–13: tax on petrol, then a lump-sum transfer just enough to
reach the OLD indifference curve at the NEW prices. Utility can be
maintained while petrol consumption falls through substitution.
This transfer does not remove the new relative price.
E2022 Q5: transfer allowing the OLD BUNDLE to be bought after removing
a food subsidy. The old bundle remains affordable; with an
old interior solution and a strictly convex indifference curve,
re-choosing on the new budget usually gives higher utility.
Old bundle affordable and old utility just reached are two
distinct compensation criteria. The general guarantee is "no worse
off"; strict improvement needs tangency/corner conditions.

4.7 Market demand
QD(p)=sum_i max(0,q_i(p)). HORIZONTAL summation at a given price.
L4 p.19: q_H=10-p, q_L=5-p.
QD=0 for p>=10; QD=10-p for 5<p<10; QD=15-2p for 0<=p<=5.
The aggregate inverse demand has a kink; check the segment of any optimum.

4.8 Behaviour, social preferences and limits
L3: utility can include altruism, status, respect, pride and spite.
Maximising utility is therefore not the same as maximising one's own consumption.
Ultimatum game: a positive money offer may be rejected for fairness.
Lack of self-control: immediate gratification against future interest.
Heuristics: simple rules when optimisation is hard.
Loss aversion: losses can weigh more than comparable gains.
Framing: how a choice is presented affects the decision.
Overconfidence: excessive confidence in one's abilities/information.
Social preferences in A3: convert money payoffs into UTILITIES
before looking for best responses.

5. ELASTICITIES — L4 AND A2
======================================================================

Elasticity of Q with respect to z: % change in Q for a 1% change
in z, locally. Discrete form in L4: (Delta Q/Q0)/(Delta z/z0).
Point form: epsilon_Q,z=(dQ/dz)*(z/Q); partial derivative if needed.

Own price: epsilon_D=(dQD/dp)*(p/QD), usually negative.
Supply: epsilon_S=(dQS/dp)*(p/QS), usually positive.
Cross price: epsilon_ij=(partial Q_i/partial p_j)*(p_j/Q_i).
Income: eta=(partial Q/partial I)*(I/Q).
Positive cross price -> substitutes; negative -> complements.
Negative income -> inferior; positive -> normal; >1 -> luxury.
Inelastic demand: -1<epsilon_D<0; unit elastic: -1; elastic: <-1.
Perfectly inelastic: 0, vertical curve. Perfectly elastic:
infinite absolute value, horizontal curve in the limiting case.

Approximate change: %Delta Q ≈ epsilon * %Delta z.
Quantity target: %Delta z ≈ %Delta Q / epsilon.
Keep the sign: a price fall and a negative epsilon give a rise in Q.
For a large change, state the convention; the linear approximation
is not an identity of a constant-elasticity function.
If Q=A*p^epsilon, exactly: Q1/Q0=(p1/p0)^epsilon.
Arc elasticity (complement): use the averages (Q0+Q1)/2 and
(p0+p1)/2 in both percentages. Do not mix conventions.

L4 examples: Q goes 6->4, p 1->2: epsilon on the initial base=-1/3.
epsilon=-1.5, price falls by 10% ->Q rises by approximately 15%.
epsilon=-0.5, target Q +5% ->price about -10%.
eta=-2, income +10% ->Q about -20%.

Linear demand Q=a-bp: derivative=-b constant, epsilon=-b*p/(a-bp)
VARIABLE. The higher p, the larger |epsilon|, for fixed parameters.
Inverse demand p=A-BQ: epsilon=-p/(B*Q).
Do not use dp/dQ instead of dQ/dp without taking the inverse.
Revenue R=pQ: dR/dp=Q*(1+epsilon_D).
Elastic demand: a price rise lowers R; inelastic: a price rise
raises R; unit elastic: revenue locally stationary.
Profit also depends on costs: maximum revenue and maximum profit
are not always at the same price.

Longer horizon -> often more substitution possibilities.
Narrow product definition -> often more substitutes.
L4 alcohol study, values from the MATERIALS, not universal rules:
Own-price spirits -1.3, beer -0.9, wine -0.2; cross-price Denmark
0.3/0.5/0.2; income 1.4/1.0/0.6; stores and Fridays 0.1/0.2/0.2.
These numbers illustrate empirical estimation, not a formula to memorise.

6. PRODUCTION, COSTS AND COMPETITIVE SUPPLY — L5–L6
======================================================================

6.1 Technology and marginal products
Function Q=f(K,L): quantity obtained with capital K and labour L.
MP_L=partial Q/partial L; MP_K=partial Q/partial K.
AP_L=Q/L, average product; discrete MP=Delta Q/Delta L, K fixed.
L5 p.11: Q=50 at L=9, 54 at L=10, 56 at L=11.
Product of the 10th worker=4; average product at 10=54/10=5.4.
Diminishing marginal returns: one input rises, the other stays fixed.
Example L5: Q=sqrt(KL), MP_L=0.5*sqrt(K/L), MP_K=0.5*sqrt(L/K).
Cobb-Douglas complement Q=A*K^alpha*L^beta:
MP_L=beta*Q/L; MP_K=alpha*Q/K.
A captures total factor productivity, TFP: more output with
the same inputs, or fewer inputs for the same output.

Hiring, output sold at a given price p: keep going while p*MP_L>w.
At a competitive interior optimum: p*MP_L=w.
With market power over output, replace p by MR (complement).
L5 Black Death: less labour relative to land can raise
the marginal product of labour and the wage, for a given technology.

6.2 Isoquants, isocosts and minimum cost
Isoquant: K,L combinations producing the same Qbar.
With L horizontal, K vertical: slope=-MP_L/MP_K.
MRTS_LK=MP_L/MP_K: capital that can be saved per unit of L added.
Isocost: C=wL+rK ->K=C/r-(w/r)L, slope=-w/r.
Minimise wL+rK subject to f(K,L)>=Qbar; equality for usual technology.
Interior optimum: MP_L/MP_K=w/r, i.e. MP_L/w=MP_K/r.
The last euro must create the same marginal output in both inputs.
Rise in w: labour relatively more expensive -> more capital-intensive
combination on the same isoquant, if substitution is possible. Minimum cost
cannot fall when the price of an input rises, other things equal.
Do not just rotate the old isocost: also find the new
tangent isocost, usually more costly, for the required Q.

Complement example for Q=sqrt(KL): K/L=w/r; KL=Qbar^2.
L*=Qbar*sqrt(r/w), K*=Qbar*sqrt(w/r), C*=2*Qbar*sqrt(wr).
For Q=K*L, isoquant K=Qbar/L, different from sqrt(KL).
E2025 Q5: isocost 1000=50L+100K ->K=10-0.5L; intercepts 20 and 10.
"Well-behaved" production does not give numerical values
of L*,K* without a more precise production function; the optimum is drawn.

6.3 Returns to scale and economies of scale
Multiply ALL inputs by t>1 and compare f(tK,tL) with t*f(K,L).
Greater -> increasing returns to scale; equal -> constant; smaller
-> decreasing. Cobb-Douglas: compare alpha+beta with 1.
Returns concern TECHNOLOGY. Economies of scale concern
long-run AVERAGE COST: ATC falls with Q. Diseconomies: ATC rises.
Diminishing marginal product of one factor does not imply decreasing
returns to scale: sqrt(KL) has diminishing MPs and constant returns.

6.4 Cost table
TC=C(Q)=FC+VC(Q).
ATC=AC=TC/Q; AFC=FC/Q; AVC=VC/Q; ATC=AFC+AVC.
MC=dTC/dQ=dVC/dQ, because the derivative of a constant FC=0.
Discrete MC of the next unit: C(Q+1)-C(Q).
MC<ATC ->ATC falls; MC>ATC ->ATC rises.
At the interior minimum of ATC: MC=ATC. Same logic for AVC.
Fixed cost does not automatically mean sunk: check whether it is
recoverable/avoidable within the decision horizon.
Short run: at least one input fixed. Long run: all adjustable,
with no universal calendar length. Long-run cost <= short-run cost
at a given Q, because it offers more choice of inputs.

Example L5 C=30+Q^2 at Q=2: TC34, FC30, VC4, ATC17, AVC2, AFC15,
continuous MC4; discrete cost of going from 2 to 3=5.
Do not confuse these two meanings of marginal cost.

6.5 Profit and supply of a price-taking firm
pi(q)=p*q-C(q). MR=p because one firm does not change the market price.
Profit maximisation is the model's assumption. L6 p.3 also invites
discussion of time horizon, other objectives and corporate social
responsibility. This does not change
a given FOC as long as no other objective is specified in the question.
FOC p=MC(q). Supply is the relevant rising part of MC above
the shutdown threshold, compared with q=0.
Example L6 C=10+q^2, p12: MC2q ->q6; R72, C46, pi26.
For any admissible p: q=p/2; at p0, q0.
Minimum ATC for this example: q=sqrt(10), price=2sqrt(10)≈6.325.
pi=(p-ATC)*q. PS=pq-VC=pi+FC; PS is not necessarily profit.

6.6 Produce or shut down?
Short run, unavoidable FC: produce if the best quantity gives
R>=VC, i.e. p>=AVC(q*); otherwise q=0 and loss=-FC.
The standard threshold is min AVC, under usual assumptions.
If AVC<p<ATC, producing at a loss can minimise the loss: part of FC
is covered. When R=VC exactly, indifferent between producing and shutting down.
Long run: stay/enter only if economic profit is >=0,
so p>=ATC at the optimal level. Otherwise exit when possible.
L6 T-shirts: MC5 up to q10, then MC15, p10 ->q10. FC25 gives pi25;
FC40 gives pi10. The change in FC does not change the interior quantity.

6.7 Market supply, entry and opportunity cost
Add individual supplies horizontally, excluding
negative quantities. L6 q1=p-2, q2=p-4:
QS=0 for p<2; p-2 for 2<=p<4; 2p-6 for p>=4.
Rise in demand: price/profits rise in the short run -> entry -> market
supply shifts right -> price/profit fall until economic profit is zero.
With identical, replicable firms at constant cost, p_LR=min ATC,
output per firm returns to the efficient level, market quantity and number of
firms rise. This result does not describe every industry.
Input scarce at industry level -> LR supply can slope up; learning
or external economies -> it can slope down.

Accounting cost: explicit spending. Economic cost: includes the value
of the best alternative forgone, without double counting.
Naomi L6: supplies 30,000 + delivery 10,000 ->accounting 40,000;
alternative salary 50,000 ->economic 90,000.
Zero economic profit implies neither zero salary nor no income:
the factors already receive their opportunity pay.
Economic rent: income from a scarce resource beyond its relevant
alternative use; different from a simple accounting rent.

7. SURPLUS, TAXES, SUBSIDIES AND REGULATED PRICES — L7
======================================================================

7.1 Who gets what?
CS, consumer surplus: total value to buyers minus their payments.
CS=integral_0^Q [p_D(q)-p_b] dq, if the units go to the buyers who
value them most. For p_D=a-bQ: CS=(a-p_b)*Q/2.
PS, producer surplus: revenue net of tax minus variable cost.
PS=integral_0^Q [p_s-MC(q)] dq; for p_S=c+dQ: PS=(p_s-c)*Q/2.
Profit=PS-FC. When prices differ, integrate the actual revenue
instead of using a single pQ rectangle.
Welfare W=CS+PS in the model without government or externality, with fixed costs
unchanged. With government: add tax revenue, subtract subsidy
spending. With an externality: add external benefits, subtract damages.
When entry or fixed costs change, also include those real costs.

A sum of surpluses measures monetary efficiency, not fairness.
A transfer from CS to PS is not by itself a DWL. A deadweight loss
is a NET loss of surplus compared with the efficient benchmark.
The competitive model maximises W if demand is the social marginal
benefit, supply the social marginal cost and the other assumptions hold.
Monopoly, an externality or asymmetric information can break this result.

L7 example: p_D=23-Q, p_S=2+2Q ->Q7,p16.
CS=(23-16)*7/2=24.5; PS=(16-2)*7/2=49; W73.5.
If Q is limited to 5 and the price is 18: CS12.5; PS55; W67.5; DWL6.
Here producers gain 6 while consumers lose 12:
the social loss is 6, not 12.

7.2 Per-unit tax: write THREE relations
QD(p_b)=QS(p_s)=Q; p_b=p_s+t.
The consumer pays p_b; the producer keeps p_s; the government receives tQ.
Whether the law makes the buyer or the seller remit the tax does not change
the equilibrium of this model, for the same tax and the same trading possibilities.

For p_D=a-bQ and p_S=c+dQ, b,d>0, interior solution:
Q0=(a-c)/(b+d); Qt=(a-c-t)/(b+d).
p_b=a-bQt; p_s=c+dQt; T=tQt.
Rise in buyer price=b*t/(b+d).
Fall in seller price=d*t/(b+d).
DWL=t*(Q0-Qt)/2=t^2/[2(b+d)], WITHOUT externality/market power.
Beyond the prohibitive tax, Q=0; do not extend the formulas to Q<0.

Economic incidence: the less elastic side bears more.
Local competitive form: buyer share=epsilon_S/(epsilon_S+|epsilon_D|),
seller share=|epsilon_D|/(epsilon_S+|epsilon_D|).
These shares are not a general rule for oligopoly or monopoly.
A less elastic base causes less change in Q, so usually
less DWL for the same levy, under the comparison assumptions.

L7 tax t3: Q6,p_b17,p_s14,T18; CS18,PS36,W72,DWL1.5.
The rectangles transferred to the government are not destroyed.
A tax can nevertheless fund a public good or correct an externality:
its DWL is not enough to judge the whole policy.

7.3 Per-unit subsidy
QD(p_b)=QS(p_s)=Q; p_s=p_b+s.
The buyer pays less, the seller receives more, the government pays sQ.
Qs=(a-c+s)/(b+d); W=CS+PS-sQ.
Without externality: DWL=s*(Qs-Q0)/2. The additional units have a real
cost above their benefit. With a positive externality, this formula
without external correction can give the wrong diagnosis.
L7 s3: Q8,p_b15,p_s18; CS32,PS64,spending24,W72,DWL1.5.

7.4 Price ceiling and floor
Price ceiling / cap: maximum price. Binding if the ceiling is below p* in the
usual case. Quantity traded often limited by QS; shortage QD-QS.
Draw the ceiling as a horizontal line; read QS and QD separately.
CS also depends on rationing: the standard formula assumes the
available units go to the buyers with the highest WTP.
Random rationing, queues and search can add losses.
PS falls; CS can rise OR fall; W falls without another failure.
L7 ceiling 12: QS5,QD11; trades 5; CS42.5,PS25,W67.5,DWL6.
A ceiling >=p* is non-binding in the simple competitive model.

Price floor: minimum price, binding above p*. Excess supply.
Without public purchases, quantity sold limited by QD; do not count QS
as quantity consumed. With public purchases, include their cost and
what happens to unsold output. The welfare result depends on this set-up.

E2023 Russian oil: do not mechanically apply "ceiling ->shortage".
Russian supply vertical beyond 50, ceiling 60 instead of 90: same volume
8 million barrels/day; rent transfer 30*8 million=240 million
USD/day. No fall in this supply under the assumptions; the ceiling
targets one supplier, not necessarily the price of the whole world market.

7.5 Taxes and revenue: a conclusion to check
Revenue=t*Q(t). Lowering t reduces the levy per unit but can raise
volume. The sign of Delta revenue is not universal without numbers.
With inelastic demand and a small tax cut, volume changes little and
revenue usually falls. Show both effects if the question
does not allow numbers. A % VAT and a per-unit tax are not
identical; E2025 explicitly allows treating VAT as per-unit.

8. MONOPOLY AND MONOPOLISTIC COMPETITION — L8
======================================================================

8.1 Single-price monopoly
One firm faces the downward-sloping market demand.
R(Q)=p(Q)*Q; MR=p(Q)+Q*p'(Q).
pi(Q)=p(Q)*Q-C(Q); interior optimum MR=MC.
Find Q with MR=MC, then find the PRICE on the DEMAND curve, not on MR.
When selling more requires lowering the price of all units,
MR<p for a strictly downward-sloping demand.
Inverse demand p=a-bQ: R=aQ-bQ^2; MR=a-2bQ, twice as steep.
Constant MC c, FC F: Qm=(a-c)/(2b), pm=(a+c)/2,
pi=(a-c)^2/(4b)-F, if producing is profitable over the horizon considered.
Efficiency without externality requires p_D=MC ->Qeff=(a-c)/b.
DWL=b*(Qeff-Qm)^2/2=(pm-c)*(Qeff-Qm)/2.

L8 p.8–11: p=12-Q, C=3+Q^2 ->MR12-2Q, MC2Q,
Qm3,pm9,ATC4,profit15; Qeff4,p_eff8; DWL1.5.
The high price transfers surplus on the units sold; the units
not sold despite WTP>MC create the deadweight loss.
A low absolute price is compatible with monopoly if MC is low and/or
demand is elastic. Example of yeast E2023 Q1a.
A monopoly has no supply curve independent of demand: MR
depends on its shape, and the same price can correspond to various optimal
quantities under various demands. Reference E2022 Q4c.

8.2 Lerner: a derivation you must be able to explain
epsilon_D=(dQ/dp)*(p/Q), so (dp/dQ)*(Q/p)=1/epsilon_D.
MR=p+Q*p'=p*(1+1/epsilon_D).
At MR=MC: (p-MC)/p=-1/epsilon_D.
Lerner L=(p-MC)/p: markup RELATIVE to price, not total profit nor (p-MC)/MC.
Large |epsilon| ->small relative markup; insensitive demand ->high markup.
With MC>0, a single-price interior optimum lies on the elastic part,
epsilon<-1; MC=0 can give epsilon=-1.
A figure labelled "inelastic demand" in the materials may compare
relative sensitivities; a monopoly with positive MC does not optimise at
a strictly inelastic point. Lowering Q there would raise R and
save costs.
This relation concerns the FIRM's demand. In oligopoly, do not
blindly substitute the market elasticity for the one the firm perceives.

8.3 Barriers and natural monopoly
Barriers: regulation/licence, patent, control of an essential input,
economies of scale/natural monopoly, strategic behaviour.
Natural monopoly: one firm can serve the market at lower cost than
several, over the relevant range. Price=MC may not cover FC.
Patent: static surplus loss versus incentives to invest/innovate,
to be compared over time. E2024 Q2b does not ask you to deny the static DWL.
The precise legal duration mentioned in an exercise is a given of
the question, not a legal check by this dossier.

8.4 Monopolistic competition
Many firms, differentiated products, easy entry/exit; a firm
faces a downward-sloping demand but usually ignores the reaction of any
specific competitor. It chooses MR=MC.
Short run: positive or negative profit possible.
Positive profit ->entry ->demand per firm falls and often becomes
more elastic ->zero economic profit in the symmetric LR model.
Long run: p=ATC BUT p>MC; zero profit does not mean perfect competition.
Typically output below the ATC-minimising quantity, "excess capacity".
The number of firms and variety are part of the equilibrium.

Negative demand shock ->initial losses ->exits ->demand of the survivors
recovers ->zero profit restored. The number of firms falls; output
per survivor has no universal sign without demand/cost functions.
If the shock only changes market size in a standard model with
constant optimal size, it can return to its previous level.
Cost shock ->re-optimisation and entry/exit; in oligopoly with barriers,
the zero-profit discipline of free entry does not apply in the same way.

8.5 Heterogeneous firms
More productive ->lower MC, often lower price, higher volume/market
share. "Competitive reallocation": the efficient grow,
the less efficient shrink/exit.
Free entry can imply E[pi]=0 BEFORE a firm discovers its productivity,
without imposing pi=0 for each firm once its type is known.
L8 p.24, entry cost 20 and a fair die: realised profits
28,16,4,-8,-20,-20; mean 0. For the worst draw, potential variable profit
-12: the firm shuts down, limiting its loss to 20. This table distinguishes
expected profit, realised profit and shutdown after a sunk entry cost.

9. DISCRIMINATION, SEGMENTATION, BUNDLING AND TARIFFS — L9
======================================================================

9.1 Conditions and three degrees
Discrimination: different units of the same good sold at different
prices, for WTP reasons beyond mere cost differences.
It requires market power and limiting resale/arbitrage; knowing how to sort
customers, or offering them a menu that makes them sort themselves.
A price difference due exclusively to cost does not on its own
establish discrimination.

First degree / perfect price discrimination: each unit is sold
at its maximum WTP. Produce up to p_D=MC if there is no other distortion.
CS0; PS captures all the variable surplus; DWL0 in this ideal model.
Profit=PS-FC. High efficiency and a split very favourable to the seller.
Requires very strong information and extraction, usually unrealistic.

Third degree: OBSERVABLE groups, for example students, retirees,
countries. Solve MR_i=MC for each group (or MC of the total if cost is not constant).
With a common MC: (p_i-MC)/p_i=-1/epsilon_i.
At the optimum, the group less sensitive in absolute value ->higher price.
An observable status is not enough to prove that the discount raises
profit: demand, capacity and opportunity cost must be considered.

Second degree: unobservable type, SELF-SELECTION by quantity, quality,
schedule, waiting, coupons, freemium, premium version or comfort.
The seller wants type H to choose offer H and type L offer L.
L9 examples: 1st/2nd class trains, books, bookings, freemium games.
Degrading the economy version can stop H customers from abandoning
the expensive version, even if this degradation saves no costs.

9.2 IC and IR constraints
Net utility of type i choosing menu j: V_i(B_j)-T_j.
IC_H: V_H(B_H)-T_H >= V_H(B_L)-T_L.
IC_L: V_L(B_L)-T_L >= V_L(B_H)-T_H.
IR_i: V_i(B_i)-T_i >= outside utility (often normalised to 0).
L9 p.20 mainly shows IC_H and IR_L, often binding in the usual
model; the others must not be forgotten without justification.
IC prevents choosing the "wrong" contract; IR prevents refusing every
contract. Prices and qualities must be chosen simultaneously under these constraints.
An information rent may have to be left to H customers.

9.3 Segmentation versus a common price
Segmented, p_i=a_i-b_i Q_i and constant MC=c:
Q_i=(a_i-c)/(2b_i); p_i=(a_i+c)/2; compare with 0 if a_i<=c.
Common price: Q(p)=sum_i max(0,(a_i-p)/b_i).
Method: find all exclusion prices, write each segment,
maximise (p-c)*Q(p) on each segment, check the boundaries,
compare the profits. It may be optimal to serve only one group.
Do not add inverse demands to obtain a common private demand.

L9 Lowies p=1-Q_L/2, Highies p=2-Q_H, MC0:
segmented QL1,pL0.5,piL0.5; QH1,pH1,piH1; pi_total1.5.
Common, in the zone where both buy: Q=4-3p,
p=2/3,Q2,pi4/3. Also check p>=1 where only Highies buy.
Discrimination raises feasible profit, because the common price remains
an option. Welfare and each group's CS have an AMBIGUOUS sign.
Opening to an excluded group can improve W; a mere reallocation
at unchanged quantity between different WTPs can lower it.
The poorer country is not automatically the more elastic: check
demand, instead of deducing prices from national income alone.
Parallel imports/trade: buy in the cheap market and resell in the expensive
market; this undermines segmentation.

9.4 Bundling
Pure bundling: only the bundle; mixed bundling: bundle AND separate purchases.
Add up the same person's WTPs to find their bundle WTP,
then count purchases at each candidate price. Compare PROFITS,
so include costs and quantities sold, not just revenues.
L9 p.22 and E2022 Q4d: two representative customers, costs not specified.
Gourmet: dinner100, room50, bundle150; Snoozer: dinner80, room70,
bundle150. With zero costs and one person of each type:
dinner price80 sold2 ->160; room price50 sold2 ->100; total260.
Bundle price150 sold2 ->300. The bundle extracts more from negatively
correlated WTPs. Without given costs, this numerical result assumes zero costs or
per-type/per-service costs that do not reverse the comparison.
Example: very high real costs of one service can change the choice.

9.5 Flat subscription and two-part tariff: an essential distinction
Two-part tariff: payment T(q)=F+p*q.
With a single known customer, constant marginal cost c and no extra
constraint: usage price p=c, efficient quantity, F=CS(p=c).
The seller extracts the surplus while giving an efficient marginal incentive.
With heterogeneous types or a common F, p=c is not always optimal.
FIXED price only: p=0; consumption up to marginal WTP 0,
F=integral_0^q(0) p_D(x)dx; profit=F-C(q(0)). This is not the two-part
solution when MC>0: excessive usage really costs.

L9 p.23–25: p_D=10-Q, C=2Q.
Simple monopoly Q4,p6,pi16.
Two-part p2,Q8,F32,pi32.
Subscription only p0,Q10,F50,cost20,pi30.
F is revenue for the firm and an expense for the customer; do not
confuse it with the fixed production cost, also often written F.

9.6 Capacity and complementary products — E2025 Q1
Capacity2000, 3000 fans WTP1200, 2000 students WTP500, MC0:
filling with 2000 fans at 1200 gives 2 400 000 kr. No seat is available
to add students without replacing a fan who pays more.
Discrimination therefore cannot raise profit in this case.
The physical marginal cost is 0, but the seat has an opportunity cost.

Concert and T-shirts: maximise JOINT profit. A cheaper ticket
can attract a buyer whose T-shirt purchases add margin.
Qc=1000-pc, QT=Qc-2pT, MCc0,MCT20:
pi=pc*(1000-pc)+(pT-20)*(1000-pc-2pT).
FOC: 1020-2pc-pT=0; 1040-pc-4pT=0.
pc=3040/7≈434.286; pT=1060/7≈151.429; Qc=3960/7≈565.714;
QT=1840/7≈262.857. Ticket alone: pc500; complementary product ->lower pc.
Check QT>=0 and the validity of the demands; here the optimum is interior.

10. OLIGOPOLY, COURNOT, BERTRAND AND STACKELBERG — L10
======================================================================

10.1 Choosing the right game
Oligopoly: a small number of firms whose decisions directly affect each other.
Cournot: quantities chosen simultaneously; homogeneous product in the base case.
Bertrand: prices chosen simultaneously; state whether homogeneous or differentiated.
Stackelberg: a leader chooses first, a follower observes and reacts.
Nash: no firm gains by deviating UNILATERALLY, the other strategies
being fixed. The intersection of best responses is a Nash equilibrium.
A best response is a choice conditional on the rival, not a promise
to hold that rival constant after every change to the model.

10.2 Linear Cournot with two firms
p=a-b(q1+q2), costs C_i=F_i+c_i*q_i.
pi1=[a-b(q1+q2)-c1]*q1-F1.
partial pi1/partial q1=a-2bq1-bq2-c1=0.
BR1(q2)=max(0,(a-c1-bq2)/(2b)), BR2 likewise.
If both are active:
q1=(a-2c1+c2)/(3b), q2=(a-2c2+c1)/(3b),
Q=(2a-c1-c2)/(3b), p=(a+c1+c2)/3.
If a quantity is negative, solve the corner where that firm does not produce.
Then compute pi_i=(p-c_i)q_i-F_i.
Inside this linear model, p-c_i=bq_i ->variable profit=bq_i^2.
Differences in unavoidable FC do not change these quantities; they change
profits and can change entry/exit decisions.

L10 example a70,b1,c1=c2=10: BR_i=30-q_j/2;
q1=q2=20,Q40,p30,pi_i400 without FC.
Joint monopoly Q30,p40; perfect competition Q60,p10.
With MC Blue0, Red10: qBlue80/3≈26.667, qRed50/3≈16.667,
p80/3≈26.667. The efficient firm takes more of the market; imposing equal
shares can make production more expensive. Large shares do not on their own
prove inefficiency.

10.3 Cournot with n identical firms
p=a-bQ, MCc: q_i=(a-c)/[b(n+1)],
Q=n(a-c)/[b(n+1)], p=(a+n*c)/(n+1).
Lerner=(p-c)/p=-(market share_i)/epsilon_market for this model,
so under symmetry -1/(n*epsilon_market).
As n grows: p tends to c, Q to the competitive quantity.
A merger reducing n, without cost synergy: p rises; the non-merged firms
often benefit from this reduced competition in this model.
Do not generalise this result to every merger with changing costs/products.

10.4 Drawing Cournot reactions
Axes q1 horizontal/q2 vertical OR the reverse, but keep the same convention.
If q1 is horizontal: BR2 is drawn directly as q2=f(q1), and BR1 must be
rewritten q2=(a-c1)/b-2q1 in the interior zone.
Both slope downward; intersection = Nash quantities.
Cost c1 rises: BR1 changes, BR2 as a function of q1 does not change;
the rival responds to the new equilibrium. A tax on both firms: shifts
BOTH reactions towards lower output. This is E2022 Q3e.

10.5 Stackelberg in quantities
First solve BR_follower(qL), substitute it into the leader's profit,
then differentiate with respect to qL. Do not treat qF as constant here.
Symmetric case p=a-bQ, MCc, no capacity constraints:
qL=(a-c)/(2b), qF=(a-c)/(4b), Q=3(a-c)/(4b), p=(a+3c)/4.
The leader produces more and earns more than under Cournot; the follower produces less.
Total Q rises, p falls. The result depends on the model's assumptions.
L10 a70,c10: qL30,qF15,Q45,p25; profits 450 and 225.
The Stackelberg equilibrium lies on the follower's reaction, usually not
on the leader's Cournot reaction, which holds the rival's quantity fixed.

10.6 Homogeneous Bertrand
Perfectly substitutable products, same constant MC, sufficient capacity,
customers buying from the cheapest: with two or more firms, p=MC.
If p>MC, undercutting slightly can capture the market; p<MC loses
money on each unit. Lerner0 for p=MC>0.
Limited capacity, differentiation, search or repetition can change
the result; the number of firms alone does not determine the markup.
Different costs: the lowest-cost firm can keep a markup by
limiting its price by the cost of the most efficient competitor.
E2024 MC5,7,8 and p_D10-Q: limit price around 7, often written 7-epsilon.
The monopoly price without a competitor would be 7.5, so the rival's constraint
is relevant. With continuous prices, existence/tie-breaking need precision:
price exactly 7 if there is a suitable rule allocating to the efficient producer;
with equal sharing at a tie, the approximation of slight undercutting is
more accurate than claiming a pure equilibrium at 7.

10.7 Differentiated Bertrand
Demand q_i=A-B*p_i+D*p_j, B>0,D>=0, MCc_i.
pi_i=(p_i-c_i)*(A-Bp_i+Dp_j)-F_i.
FOC: A-2Bp_i+Dp_j+B*c_i=0.
BR_i(p_j)=(A+B*c_i+D*p_j)/(2B).
Upward-sloping reactions for substitutes: prices are strategic complements.
Under symmetry: p=(A+B*c)/(2B-D), if domain/concavity are admissible.
Quantity via the DEMAND; profit=(p-c)q-F.
L10 A85,B1,D0.5,c11 ->p64,q53,pi2809 without FC.
E2025 A10,B2,D1: p=(10+2c)/3.
c2 ->p14/3; c1 ->p4. Cost falls by 1 ->price falls by 2/3.
Do not apply competitive pass-through to this market structure.

10.8 Price leadership
Substitute the follower's PRICE reaction into the leader's demand,
before maximising its profit. Not the Stackelberg quantity formula.
E2024 Q4c/d: q1=6-2p1+p2, q2=6-2p2+p1; no cost is specified.
Under the explicit assumption MC=FC=0: simultaneous p1=p2=2, q1=q2=4, pi_i8.
If firm2 leads: p1=(6+p2)/4; demand2=7.5-1.75p2;
p2=15/7≈2.143, p1=57/28≈2.036; pi2=225/28≈8.036,
pi1=3249/392≈8.288. BOTH gain here; the follower gains more.
The "first-mover advantage" in quantities is not a universal law
for all games, especially when prices are complements.

11. EXTERNALITIES, POLLUTION, PUBLIC GOODS AND COMMONS — L11
======================================================================

11.1 Private, external, social
Externality: an effect on a third party, not taken into account in the transaction.
Negative: pollution, noise; positive: vaccination, research, network.
MPC / PMC = marginal private cost.
MEC = marginal external cost, the additional DAMAGE.
MSC / SMC = marginal social cost = MPC+MEC.
MPB / PMB = marginal private benefit.
MEB = marginal external benefit, the additional external benefit.
MSB / SMB = marginal social benefit = MPB+MEB.
The notation MC_p and MC_e in E2024 means MPC and MEC respectively.
MEC(Q) is a MARGINAL cost; TOTAL external cost is its integral,
not MEC(Q) itself. If MEC is constant e, total damage=eQ.
If MEC(Q)=Q, total damage=Q^2/2, not Q^2.

Private competitive equilibrium: MPB=MPC.
Social optimum: MSB=MSC.
Negative production externality alone: demand=MPB=MSB and supply=MPC;
the market produces too much. Positive consumption externality alone:
demand=MPB, MSB above it; the market produces too little.
The universal rule is MSB=MSC; the production/consumption distinction
determines which side of the graph to adjust.

11.2 Measuring W and DWL
W(Q)=integral_0^Q [MSB(q)-MSC(q)]dq, minus relevant real fixed costs.
DWL=W(Qeff)-W(Qobserved): area between MSB and MSC over the gap interval.
With a negative externality: W=CS+PS+tax revenue-total external damage.
With a positive one: W=CS+PS-subsidy spending+total external benefit.
The tax/subsidy is a transfer; damages/benefits are real effects
on third parties. The "societal producer surplus" of L11 includes
third-party damages; it differs from the private PS of the firms alone.

L11 p.5–8 and E2023: MPB=100-Q,MPC=10+Q,MEC=Q,MSC=10+2Q.
Private: Q45,p55. Social: Q30,MPB70,MPC40,MEC30.
Unregulated DWL=0.5*(45-30)*45=337.5.
At Q45: MSC100,MSB55; height 45. At the social optimum, a buyer price 70 and a
seller price 40 can be linked by the optimal tax 30.

11.3 Pigou and imperfect intervention
Corrective tax t*=MEC(Qeff), for a negative externality
not already internalised and an otherwise competitive market.
Corrective subsidy s*=MEB(Qeff), the analogous positive case.
Evaluate the marginal value at the EFFICIENT QUANTITY, not automatically
at the initial quantity. If the externality depends on Q, t* is not arbitrary.
A tax can reduce a pre-existing DWL. An excessive subsidy can
overshoot Qeff and recreate a DWL. Always compare with the social optimum, not the private one.

Negative example above, tax 20: 100-Q=10+Q+20 ->Q35,
p_b65,p_s45. Q stays above 30: DWL=0.5*5*15=37.5.
Optimal tax 30 ->Q30,DWL0 in this model.
E2024 textiles: demand200-Q,MPC80+Q,MECQ ->Qpriv60,Qeff40,
DWL0.5*20*60=600; optimal tax 40.
A6 solar: MPB100-3Q,MPC10+2Q,MEB20 ->Qpriv18,p46,
MSB120-3Q ->Qeff22, subsidy 20; uncorrected DWL40.
Buyer price at Q22=34; seller 54; gap 20.

11.4 Pollution and abatement: look at the horizontal axis
MAC=marginal abatement cost, the marginal cost of reducing pollution.
If the axis is ABATEMENT a: MAC often rises as a rises.
If the axis is remaining POLLUTION e: the MAC of the next reduction is
often decreasing in e. The two graphs are compatible.
Optimum: cost of reducing one unit = damage of that unit, MAC=MEC.
Emissions tax: the firm chooses MAC=t; optimal tax=MEC at the optimum.
L11 p.13: on the pollution axis Q, MAC=10-Q, MEC=Q/2.
Without tax Q10; tax 2 ->MAC2 ->Q8, MEC4. This is not the optimum:
optimum Q=20/3≈6.667, tax 10/3≈3.333.
A tax per unit of OUTPUT is equivalent to a tax per unit of EMISSION only if
emission per unit is fixed; otherwise taxing emissions directly can
also encourage cleaner technology.

11.5 Quotas, permits and uncertainty
Quota: fixes a quantity of emissions. Tax: fixes the price of polluting.
Tradable permits: a total cap and transferable rights.
If MAC_L<MAC_H at the initial allocation, L abates more and sells
permits; H buys and abates less. There is a gain as long as MACs differ.
At an interior solution, MAC_L=MAC_H=permit price: the same cap reached at
minimum cost. The initial allocation affects rents, not necessarily
the efficient final allocation under frictionless trading.
An identical quota per firm does not generally minimise total cost.

E2021 Q1d, "prices versus quantities" complement: uncertain abatement
costs and very steep marginal damages ->a quota/cap protects better
against a large error in the quantity of emissions. Relatively flat marginal
damages and steep MAC ->a tax can better avoid an excessive cost
of complying with the quota. An argument about RELATIVE slopes around the optimum,
not a rule "quotas are always better". Draw two possible MACs.

11.6 Coase and property rights
Well-defined rights and costless bargaining ->the parties can negotiate
an efficient quantity, whatever the initial allocation of rights,
under the theorem's assumptions. The allocation changes who pays and incomes.
Intuition: gains from an agreement when reducing the damage is worth more
than the profit it costs; bargain until the gain disappears.
Transaction costs: time, contracts, monitoring, a large number of parties,
free riders, coordination and social norms. They can block
agreements. Uncertain rights ->the theorem's condition is not met.
Bringing polluter and victim under one owner can internalise
the effect, but an owner who becomes a monopolist can introduce another
distortion; do not automatically conclude the socially optimal
competitive quantity. E2024 Q3d and E2025 Q1c.

11.7 Four categories of goods
Excludable: access can be prevented. Rival: one person's use
reduces the possibilities of others.
                       Excludable                 Non-excludable
Rival                  Private good               Common resource
Non-rival              Club good                  Pure public good
Private: sandwich. Club: uncongested subscription/service.
Common: open-access fishery; spectrum with interference (E2023).
Public: defence, disseminated basic research, street cleaning
in the exercise's model. Congestion and access rules can
change the classification. A road/tunnel is not always a pure public
good: tolls are possible and there is rivalry when congested.

11.8 Public good: VERTICAL summation
Everyone benefits from the same Q. MB_social(Q)=sum_i max(0,MB_i(Q)).
Interior optimum: sum MB_i=MC, the Samuelson condition (standard name,
not necessarily used in the slides).
Truncate each negative WTP at 0 if the good is simply not wanted beyond
a quantity; if the good really does harm, the model changes.
Identify the thresholds where each MB reaches zero, then check the segment.
Free rider: benefiting even without paying ->incentive to under-contribute and
to under-reveal one's WTP. Voluntary provision is usually insufficient,
not necessarily zero. Compulsory taxes/norms can help.

L11 p.25: MB_L=10-h, MB_H=20-h.
For h<=10: sum 30-2h. MC26 ->h2; MC14 ->h8.
At MC14, H alone would fund h6, below the collective optimum 8.
At MC26, no individual alone funds anything even though h2 is collectively useful.
E2024 tunnel: MB1=10-Q,MB2=5-Q,MB3=10-2Q,MC9.
On Q<=5, sum 25-4Q; Qeff4, all WTPs positive at 4.
E2025/A6 cleaning: MB15-Q,12-Q,9-Q; sum 36-3Q; MC27 ->Qeff3.
E2022 wifi: 300 people MB10-Q; 300(10-Q)=1500 ->Qeff5.
Do not write Q=300(10-p) for this public good: each person consumes
the SAME speed, not an individual fraction of the speed.

11.9 Common resource and open access
Each user imposes on others a scarcity/congestion they ignore.
Tragedy of the commons: overuse, not a lack of value of the resource.
L11 fishery example: average revenue per boat vs individual cost; entry
until average revenue=cost, whereas the optimum uses the SOCIAL marginal
product of an extra boat. The total catch can deteriorate
with too many boats. Do not use MP and AP as synonyms.
Solutions: property rights, quantity restrictions, permits,
fees, local rules and norms in repeated interactions.
Free rider of the public good = under-contribution; commons = overexploitation.

12. ASYMMETRIC INFORMATION, CONTRACTS AND INCENTIVES — L12
======================================================================

12.1 Identifying what is hidden
Asymmetric information: one party knows something the other does not.
Adverse selection: private TYPE/quality/risk, often before the contract.
Moral hazard: private ACTION/effort, often after the contract.
The presence of risk alone is not an information asymmetry.
The central diagnosis: who knows what, at what moment, and which choice does it affect?

12.2 Lemons / adverse selection
L12 cars: the buyer values high quality at 150, low at 50; the seller
high 130, low 30; initially the probability of high is 0.5.
Observable quality: trades possible in [130,150] and [30,50].
Quality unknown to EVERYONE: average WTP 100, average seller reservation 80;
trades between 80 and 100 possible under the model's risk neutrality.
Only the seller informed: an average price of 100 does not make good cars sell
(reservation 130). They leave; average quality falls; buyers revise
their WTP; the high-end market can disappear despite potential gains.
Beliefs must be CONSISTENT with the sellers who remain at the offered price.

L12 driving-licence insurance: those who expect to fail more
may buy more, so the insured's risk exceeds the 52% average
under adverse selection; the contract can also change effort (moral hazard).
Reputation, warranties, certification, quality rules, the right of return,
shared information, group contracts and compulsory insurance can
reduce adverse selection; their effectiveness depends on the mechanism.
E2020 Klarna, E2023 customer reviews, E2024 wine standards: a certification
or collective reputation can allow small brands to guarantee
their quality and reduce the informational advantage of large brands.

12.3 Screening and signalling
Screening: the LESS informed party collects/tests/structures a menu to
learn the type. Signalling: the INFORMED party takes an observable
action to reveal its type.
A credible signal must be costly enough to imitate for the bad
type, relative to the benefit obtained; "costly" alone is not enough.
L12 education: wage 100 with a degree, 60 without; cost C_A<C_S.
Separation if 100-C_A>60 and 100-C_S<60, so C_A<40<C_S.
At equalities, types may be indifferent; specify the selection.
Pooling: types choose the same signal; separating: different signals.
A degree's signalling value does not prove that it creates no skills.

12.4 Principal-agent and risk sharing
The principal offers the contract; the agent chooses an effort that is not perfectly
observable. The outcome depends on effort AND on shocks.
Rewarding the outcome gives incentives but exposes the agent to risk.
Fixed wage: weak monetary incentive to effort if effort is costly.
Revenue sharing: the agent keeps part of the gain from effort and of the risk.
The agent pays a fixed fee and keeps the revenue: strong incentives
at the margin, but the agent bears the risk. Franchise/business lease.
Risk, limited liability and wealth can make this contract difficult.

L12 p.13–15, verified table: probability 0.5 bad/good state.
Effort H, private cost 10: revenues 40 or 100 ->expected revenue 70,
total net surplus 60. Effort L, cost 0: revenues 20 or 40 ->expectation 30.
Fixed wage w independent of revenue: the agent prefers L; receives w,
the owner receives 30-w; the owner bears the revenue risk.
50/50 sharing: H gives the agent 0.5*70-10=25, the owner 35;
L gives the agent 15 ->H chosen. Risk shared.
Fixed fee F to the owner, the agent keeps the revenue: H gives the agent 60-F,
the owner F; L 30-F ->H. The agent bears the revenue risk.
Participation also requires the agent to prefer the contract to their
outside option; a good incentive is not enough to get the contract accepted.

12.5 Credit with hidden action: general method
The project costs I0, revenue R if it succeeds, 0 otherwise. Loan D, repayment Bk
only if it succeeds, limited liability. Success p_H with effort
H and p_L with L, p_H>p_L. L yields a private benefit b.
Entrepreneur's utility/expectation under H=p_H*(R-Bk).
Entrepreneur's utility under L=p_L*(R-Bk)+b.
If own funds A are invested up front, subtract A in BOTH cases
when measuring the net gain from before investment; it cancels out
in the effort comparison for a given contract.
IC_H: (p_H-p_L)*(R-Bk)>=b.
Bank's expected profit=p_chosen*Bk-D, with bank rates/costs normalised to 0.
Financing requires bank profit>=0 AND the H incentive if the calculation relies on H,
as well as the entrepreneur's participation given their outside option.
A repayment that is too high can destroy effort, lower p and reduce the
EXPECTED repayment despite a higher nominal repayment.

A6/E2023: R40,pH0.8,pL0.4,D20,Bk30.
H8; L4+b. b2 ->H8>L6, bank 24-20=4.
b6 ->L10>H8, bank 12-20=-8.
Own funds 10, D10,Bk15: H20; L10+b=12 or 16 ->H for b2 AND b6;

12.6 Contracts, monitoring and penalty
E2025 cleaning: payment 110; high-quality cost 100; low 70.
Profit H 10; profit L 40-0.5*F. H at least as good if F>=60.
F=60 gives indifference; F>60 makes H strictly preferred.
Formula: minimum penalty=(private cost saving)/(probability of detection),
if the penalty is payable, the agent is risk-neutral and the contract has no other effect.
The payment of 110 cancels out in the comparison; it matters for participation.

Carpenter E2022: a fixed fee encourages saving time/cost, but risks
sacrificing hidden quality; hourly pay protects against the risk of
duration but can encourage stretching the hours. Many other customers
raise the opportunity cost of time and can limit this incentive.
Tipping E2023: the customer observes service quality better than the boss;
a tip can give the waiter a link between quality and pay.
Insurance deductible: the insured bears part of the cost and
keeps an incentive to avoid the loss; an insurance/incentive trade-off.
Private equity: monitoring, information and involvement can reduce
some hidden-action problems; do not automatically conflate
bank financing and equity financing.

12.7 Asymmetric bonus: do not assume the diagnosis before calculating
E2020 Q4d: A yields 100 with 0.75 and 80 with 0.25 ->E profit 95.
B yields 400 with 0.75 and -160 with 0.25 ->E profit 260.
Bonus 1% of positive profits, 0 if a loss: expectation 0.95 for A, 3 for B.
The manager prefers B, and B also maximises gross expected profit here;
net of the bonus, 94.05 versus 257. On these data alone and under risk
neutrality, the bonus creates no conflict over the A/B choice. A convex
payment can favour excessive risk with OTHER numbers, but
this question does not show it. Be careful if bankruptcy constraints
or risk preferences are added: they are not quantified here.

13. GAME THEORY IN ASSIGNMENT 3

13.1 Solving a matrix
A pure strategy is a determined choice. A strategy in a sequential
game describes the action at EVERY possible node, even off the path played.
In a matrix: first payoff for the row player, second for the column player.
For each column, mark the row player's best responses; for each
row, the column player's. A doubly marked cell is a pure Nash equilibrium.
There can be several, including with indifferences.
Dominant: optimal whatever the rivals' actions; strictly
dominant if always strictly better. An equilibrium can be
inefficient: no unilateral gain does not imply maximal collective gain.

13.2 Golden Balls — data A3 p.1, matrix checked visually
              Colin S          Colin N
Rowena S      (10,10)           (0,20)
Rowena N      (20,0)            (0,0)
S=Split, N=Steal in the file; the symbol N is not universal.
Selfish: N is weakly dominant; pure Nash (S,N),(N,S),(N,N).
Envy: subtract 1 from one's utility if the other receives more.
SS(10,10), SN(-1,20), NS(20,-1), NN(0,0) ->only Nash NN.
Equal altruism: u_i=money_i+money_j.
SS gives utilities (20,20), SN(20,20), NS(20,20), NN(0,0).
Pure Nash SS,SN,NS. NN is NOT one: deviating to S against N gives 20
instead of 0. The personal answer "SS and NN" in A3.1c is wrong.
Double altruism: u_i=money_i+2*money_j.
SS(30,30), SN(40,20), NS(20,40), NN(0,0) ->S strictly dominant,
only Nash SS. Different preferences ->a different game in utilities.
These lists concern PURE strategies; do not present them as
a complete classification of all mixed equilibria.
Complement for classifying all Nash equilibria of this matrix:
let x be the probability that Rowena chooses S, y that Colin chooses S.
Selfish: equilibria if x=0 OR y=0; the other can mix arbitrarily.
Envy: x=y=0 only. Equal altruism: x=1 OR y=1; the other can
mix arbitrarily. Double altruism: x=y=1 only.
At the values 0/1 we recover exactly the pure Nash equilibria above.

13.3 Arms and oil: backward induction
Oil pool 6; arms cost X1,Y3. If only one is armed, split 5/1; if neither,
3/3; if both are armed, no oil but the costs are paid.
Net payoffs (X,Y): neither armed (3,3), X alone (4,1), Y alone (1,2), both (-1,-3).
X moves first: Y chooses not to arm after X arms (1>-3).
If X does not arm, Y compares Y armed 2 with Y unarmed 3 ->also does not arm.
So Y's complete strategy: do not arm after each of X's actions.
X compares arming 4 and not arming 3 ->arms; outcome (4,1).
Y moves first: X chooses not to arm after Y arms (1>-1), and
to arm after Y does not arm (4>3). Y compares arming 2 and not arming 1 ->arms;
outcome (1,2). The follower X would stay unarmed against an armed Y, unlike
an answer that confuses its payoff 1 with its arming cost.
A SPNE also specifies off-path reactions, not just the outcome.

13.4 Peace/war coordination — A3 p.3
                 Y peace      Y war
X peace          (9,9)        (0,8)
X war            (8,0)        (7,7)
Pure Nash peace/peace and war/war. Peace/peace Pareto-dominates.
Fear of the other and beliefs can make war/war plausible,
but payoffs alone do not select a unique simultaneous outcome.
If X moves first: Y answers peace to peace (9>8), war to war (7>0).
X anticipates 9 versus 7 ->peace; outcome peace/peace, SPNE by induction.
Communication can coordinate beliefs; non-binding cheap talk
does not necessarily change incentives.

13.5 Commitments and credible threats — A3.4
To analyse a promised sanction: at the moment it should be
carried out, does the one who promised still have an interest in honouring it?
A public written text can impose reputational/political costs, without guaranteeing
an irrevocable commitment. A military clause deters only if it
changes incentives and/or makes intervention credible after the attack.
The assignment's historical question serves as a theoretical application;
this dossier does not claim to verify the document's diplomatic history.

14.1 Absolute and comparative advantage
Absolute: fewer inputs needed per unit. Comparative: lower
opportunity cost relative to the other good.
If a_X hours/unit of X and a_Y hours/unit of Y: opportunity cost of X
in Y =a_X/a_Y. Do not invert this ratio when switching from hours to quantities.
Two countries can gain from trade even if one is more productive in
EVERYTHING. A relative trading price lying between their opportunity costs ->
possible gains. Demand/capacity constraints limit specialisation.
Linear PPF: a_X*X+a_Y*Y=Lbar; intercepts Lbar/a_X and Lbar/a_Y,
slope dY/dX=-a_X/a_Y. Curved PPF under other technologies.

E2025: Northland wheat 3h, electronics 6h; Southland wheat 4h, electronics 2h.
Southland has the absolute advantage in electronics; Northland the comparative advantage in wheat
(0.5 electronics/wheat versus 2). With 120h:
North PPF: 3W+6E=120, intercepts W40,E20; South: 4W+2E=120, W30,E60.
Trading 1 electronics for a quantity of wheat between 0.5 and 2, or equivalently
1 wheat for electronics between 0.5 and 2 here, allows potential gains.
Careful: the numerical symmetry of these intervals is specific to these numbers.

14.2 Small country, free trade and tariff
World price pw given; domestic price=pw under free trade and with no
other relevant cost. Imports M=QD(pw)-QS(pw)>0.
Unit tariff tau, the country remaining an importer: p_dom=pw+tau.
QD falls, QS rises, M falls. CS falls, domestic PS rises,
the state receives tau*M. NATIONAL welfare=CS+PS+revenue; spending on
foreign exporters does not become domestic national surplus.
DWL=triangle of inefficient production+triangle of lost consumption.
Linear: DWL=0.5*tau*[(QS1-QS0)+(QD0-QD1)].
If the tariff exceeds the gap between the autarky price and pw, it is prohibitive:
the domestic price reaches autarky, M0, do not apply pw+tau blindly.
The large-country case/effect on the world price is not the benchmark above.

E2022: pD80-Q,pS20+Q,pw30 ->QD50,QS10,M40.
Tariff 10 ->p40,QD40,QS20,M20; CS1250->800,PS50->200,
revenue 200,DWL100. Check -450+150+200=-100.
E2025 steel: see figures and unit conversion in section 16.

14.3 Specific factors and distribution
Capital specific to manufacturing, land specific to agriculture,
mobile labour. Each sector hires according to p_j*MP_Lj=w; mobility
equalises wages, under the model's competitive assumptions.
The agricultural price falls ->the value of the agricultural marginal product falls ->
labour leaves agriculture ->land income falls; capital in
the other sector can gain from the inflow of labour. The effect on labour's real wage
depends on the basket consumed and on both prices.
Food-importing country (E2020): a lower import price improves
its terms of trade and can raise aggregate welfare, despite losses
for landowners. Agricultural exporting country (E2025): a lower price
received worsens the terms of trade and hurts landowners.
PPF graph: production where relative price=MRT in absolute value; trade
budget line tangent to the PPF; consumption on an indifference curve reachable
with this budget. Production and consumption can differ thanks to trade.
Do not take the aggregate gain as proof that every group gains.

14.4 Platforms and network effects
Platform: facilitates interaction between groups (renters/owners,
audience/artists, buyers/sellers). Its value depends on the participants and
on the matching rules, not just on a digital catalogue.
Direct network effect: more users on the same side raise the
value for a user, e.g. a multiplayer game.
Indirect/cross effect: more of one side attracts the other, e.g. artists/audience.
Chicken-and-egg: without owners few renters, without renters few
owners. Subsidising one side or freemium can help get started.
Freemium can simultaneously sort WTPs AND build a user base.
Other ingredients: trust, reviews, payment, guarantee, transaction
security, matching liquidity, low friction, rules and quality.
Multi-homing: using several platforms. Single-homing: only one.
Easy multi-homing lowers an entrant's cost of reaching customers
already present elsewhere; exclusivity and switching costs can strengthen
barriers. Network effects can favour concentration/tipping;
they do not prove that every digital market is a natural monopoly.
E2022 Q1d sports rental, E2025 Q1a/b concerts.

14.5 Auctions and public procurement
Vickrey: sealed-bid second-price auction, sale of one object, independent private
values in the base model. Everyone has a weakly dominant incentive
to bid their value: the bid determines whether they win, the price is set by the
others. The highest value wins and pays the second-highest bid.
E2023 values 80,100,92 million ->player 2 wins, pays 92 million.
Overbidding can mean winning at a price above one's value; underbidding
can mean losing a profitable trade.
Reverse second-price procurement: the lowest cost/bid wins, receives
the second-lowest bid, assuming the same quality and private data.
E2025 costs 20 000,24 000,28 000 ->A wins and receives 24 000.

Common value / uncertain common cost: bids can reveal
information about the same value or the same cost. Winner's curse: winning
may mean having been the most optimistic; a rational bidder takes
this selection into account. In procurement, there is a risk of underestimating
the cost and winning a loss-making contract ->revise the bid upwards
relative to a naive estimate, if private signals are correlated.
E2025 specifies a common weather shock without specifying private information.
If everyone has exactly the same public distribution and no private
signal, the common shock alone does NOT prove an informational winner's curse.
The rule "bid your certain cost" does not carry over mechanically;
signals, risk aversion and contractual exposure must be specified.

14.6 Edgeworth and Pareto efficiency — E2021 Q4e
Edgeworth box: dimensions = total endowments of the two goods;
opposite origins for the two consumers. An allocation determines
each person's consumption, the totals being fixed.
Pareto improvement: at least one better off, nobody worse off.
Pareto efficient: no such improvement is feasible.
Gains from trade if their MRS differ, with goods available.
Under smooth preferences and an interior optimum: MRS_A=MRS_B; but with
perfect substitutes, efficient allocations are often on the edges.

E2021: totals 20 drinks D and 20 snacks S; each endowed with (10,10).
Christina U_C=D_C+S_C; Sebastian U_S=D_S+2S_S, as he gives up 2 drinks
for 1 snack. Sebastian values snacks relatively more.
Beneficial trade: Sebastian gives drinks for snacks, at a price
between 1 and 2 drinks/snack. In Sebastian's coordinates (D,S), initial utility 30,
Christina's initial 20; weak gains for both if D+2S>=30 and D+S<=20,
inside the box, with at least one strict inequality for a real gain.
Efficient allocations: Sebastian has no drinks left (D=0), OR he owns
all the snacks (S=20); otherwise a marginal trade improves both.
Among the allocations reachable without loss from the endowment,
the efficient segment D=0,15<=S<=20 satisfies both constraints.

17. CROSS-CUTTING FORMULA SHEET: WHAT, WHEN AND WHY
======================================================================

Parameter names change from one exercise to another. Each line assumes
the domains and conditions described in the previous chapters.

F01 Competitive equilibrium: QD(p*)=QS(p*).
    Equalises the quantities wanted on both sides at the same price.
F02 Inverse of a demand Q=a-bp: p=a/b-Q/b, b>0.
    Used to draw and to differentiate revenue when choosing Q.
F03 Budget: p_x*x+p_y*y<=I; slope with x horizontal=-p_x/p_y.
    A spending constraint, distinct from the objective U.
F04 MU_x=partial U/partial x; MRS_xy=MU_x/MU_y.
    Slope of the indifference curve=-MRS_xy when y is vertical.
F05 Interior consumer optimum: MRS_xy=p_x/p_y and budget exhausted.
    Equalises marginal utility per euro.
F06 Cobb-Douglas U=x^alpha*y^beta: budget shares alpha/(alpha+beta)
    and beta/(alpha+beta). Do not use for perfect substitutes/complements.
F07 Price elasticity: epsilon=(partial Q/partial p)*(p/Q).
    The derivative turns a price change into a quantity change; p/Q
    converts these changes into unit-free percentages.
F08 Approximation %Delta Q≈epsilon*%Delta p; target %Delta p≈%Delta Q/epsilon.
    Exact constant-elasticity: Q1/Q0=(p1/p0)^epsilon.
F09 Cross-price: epsilon_ij=(partial Q_i/partial p_j)*p_j/Q_i.
    Positive sign for substitutes, negative for complements.
F10 Income: eta=(partial Q/partial I)*I/Q.
    Sign and magnitude classify inferior, normal, necessity and luxury.
F11 MP_L=partial f/partial L; AP_L=Q/L.
    Marginal and average answer two different questions.
F12 Isoquant: dK/dL=-MP_L/MP_K; isocost K=C/r-(w/r)L.
F13 Interior minimum cost: MP_L/MP_K=w/r and f(K,L)=Qbar.
    The same marginal output per euro of input.
F14 Returns: compare f(tK,tL) with t*f(K,L).
    Cobb-Douglas: compare the sum of the exponents with 1.
F15 TC=FC+VC; ATC=TC/Q; AVC=VC/Q; AFC=FC/Q; MC=TC'.
    MC comes from the cost of the marginal unit, not from average cost.
F16 pi=R-C; MR=R'; interior optimum MR=MC.
    Equalises extra gain and extra cost, after checking the optimum.
F17 Price taker: MR=p ->p=MC; shut down if best R<VC in the short run.
F18 pi=(p-ATC)Q; PS=pQ-VC=pi+FC.
    Do not automatically equate PS and pi.
F19 CS=integral_0^Q p_D(q)dq-p_bQ.
F20 PS=p_sQ-integral_0^Q MC(q)dq.
F21 W=CS+PS+taxes-subsidies+external benefits-external damages,
    with consistent treatment of fixed costs when they change.
F22 Tax: p_b-p_s=t; subsidy: p_s-p_b=s; same Q on both sides.
F23 Linear p_D=a-bQ,p_S=c+dQ: Q0=(a-c)/(b+d),
    Qt=(a-c-t)/(b+d), Qs=(a-c+s)/(b+d), in the interior domain.
F24 Tax DWL with no other distortion=0.5*t*(Q0-Qt).
    Subsidy DWL without externality=0.5*s*(Qs-Q0).
F25 Monopoly: MR=p+Qp'; if p=a-bQ, MR=a-2bQ.
    The term Qp' is the revenue lost on the units already sold.
F26 Monopoly with constant MCc: Qm=(a-c)/(2b),pm=(a+c)/2.
    Price read on demand, quantity found with MR=MC.
F27 Lerner=(p-MC)/p=-1/epsilon_firm.
    The firm's demand and an interior single-price optimum.
F28 Segmentation: MR_i=MC; common aggregation Q(p)=sum max(0,q_i(p)).
F29 Known two-part tariff: p_usage=MC,F=CS(p_usage), in the simple case.
F30 Cournot: BR_i=(a-c_i-bq_j)/(2b), truncated at 0.
    Both active: q_i=(a-2c_i+c_j)/(3b).
F31 Cournot with n symmetric firms: q_i=(a-c)/[b(n+1)],p=(a+nc)/(n+1).
F32 Symmetric quantity Stackelberg: qL=(a-c)/(2b),qF=(a-c)/(4b).
    Recover it by substituting the follower's reaction.
F33 Differentiated Bertrand q_i=A-Bp_i+Dp_j:
    BR_i=(A+B*c_i+D*p_j)/(2B); symmetric p=(A+B*c)/(2B-D).
F34 MSC=MPC+MEC; MSB=MPB+MEB; social optimum MSB=MSC.
F35 Pigou: t*=MEC(Qeff) or s*=MEB(Qeff), simple competitive cases.
F36 Pollution: MAC(e)=t; optimum MAC(e)=MEC(e).
F37 Public good: sum_i MB_i(Q)=MC(Q), vertical sum and segments.
F38 Expectation: E[X]=sum p_s*X_s, net outcomes by state.
F39 Credit IC: (pH-pL)*(R-repayment)>=private benefit of L.
    Bank: p_action*repayment-loan>=0, in a model with no alternative interest.
F40 Inspection: penalty >= private saving/probability of detection.
F41 Opportunity cost of X in Y=a_X/a_Y if a is hours per unit.
F42 Import tariff: p_dom=pw+tau, imports=QD-QS, revenue=tau*imports.
    DWL=0.5*tau*(rise in QS+fall in QD), small country remaining an importer.
F43 Vickrey sale: the highest bid wins, pays the second highest.
    Procurement: the lowest bid wins, receives the second lowest.
F44 Public and private goods: the same individual functions can call for
    OPPOSITE sums depending on rivalry; do not choose by reflex.

18. DIRECTORY OF ABBREVIATIONS AND SYMBOLS
======================================================================

Marking: [O] abbreviation/symbol observed in the PDFs; [A] standard alias
of a notion explicitly present but not necessarily written as such;
[C] standard complement added to make the methods understandable.
Variants are grouped. This directory covers the corpus and its useful
variants, not every abbreviation in every microeconomics textbook.

18.1 Costs, revenue, production and profit
AC [O] — Average Cost — average cost; generally equivalent to ATC here,
  C(Q)/Q. Check whether an author uses it for another average cost.
AFC [O] — Average Fixed Cost — average fixed cost; FC/Q.
AP, APL, AP_L [A] — Average Product (of Labor) — average product (of labour); Q/L.
ATC [O] — Average Total Cost — average total cost; TC/Q.
AVC [O] — Average Variable Cost — average variable cost; VC/Q.
C(Q), TC [O] — (Total) Cost — total cost function, FC+VC(Q).
FC [O] — Fixed Cost — fixed cost; independent of Q over the horizon studied.
F [O] — Fixed cost OU Fixed fee — fixed production cost OR fee
  collected. Identify the role; the two have opposite signs in pi.
MC [O] — Marginal Cost — marginal cost; dC/dQ, money/unit of output.
MP [O] — Marginal Product — marginal product of a factor, output/input.
MPL, MP_L [O] — Marginal Product of Labor — marginal product of labour.
MPK, MP_K [A] — Marginal Product of Capital — marginal product of capital.
MR [O] — Marginal Revenue — marginal revenue; dR/dQ.
MRP_L [C] — Marginal Revenue Product of Labor — marginal revenue brought
  by an extra unit of labour input, MR*MP_L.
VMP_L [A] — Value of Marginal Product of Labor — value of the marginal
  product of labour, p*MP_L; competitive hiring condition.
CSR [A] — Corporate Social Responsibility — the firm's social
  responsibility, raised in the discussion of the profit objective in L6.
R(Q), Revenue [O] ; TR [A] — Total Revenue — total revenue, p(Q)*Q.
AR [C] — Average Revenue — average revenue, R/Q; equal to the price under a single price.
VC [O] — Variable Cost — variable cost, depends on Q.
pi, Π, π [O] — Profit — profit/economic profit depending on the costs included.
A, TFP [A] — Total Factor Productivity — total factor productivity,
  efficiency in Q=A*f(K,L). A is not always a technology parameter.
K [O] — Capital — productive capital; does not automatically mean money.
L [O] — Labor — labour; BUT L can also be the Low type or Lunches.
J [O] — Land dans L5 — land; letter chosen for this example.
w [O] — Wage — wage/cost per unit of labour.
r [O] — Rental rate of capital — rental price of one unit of capital.
q_i [O] — Firm i's quantity — quantity produced by firm i.
Q [O] — Total quantity/output — market quantity or output; read the context.
Qbar, Q avec barre [O] — imposed target output, not a freely chosen variable.
HP [O] — Highly Productive — highly productive firm, L8.
LP [O] — Less Productive — less productive firm, L8.
SR, LR [A] — Short Run, Long Run — short run/long run.
SRAC, LRAC [A] — Short-/Long-Run Average Cost — average cost by horizon.
RTS [A] — Returns To Scale — returns to scale.
IRS, CRS, DRS [A] — Increasing/Constant/Decreasing Returns to Scale —
  increasing/constant/decreasing returns; distinguish from cost economies.

18.2 Demand, consumer and elasticities
p, P [O] — Price — price per unit. Upper/lower case has no universal
  meaning: the same material may vary its notation.
QD, Q^D, Q_D [O] — Quantity Demanded — quantity demanded at a price.
QS, Q^S, Q_S [O] — Quantity Supplied — quantity supplied at a price.
D, S [O] — Demand, Supply — demand/supply curves on graphs.
  D and S can also denote a loan, a player or a group elsewhere.

20.1 Corrections and ambiguities not to reproduce
1) A3.1c, equal altruistic preferences: pure Nash SS,SN,NS; NN is wrong.
2) A3.2c: if Y alone is armed, X receives 1, Y receives 5-3=2. Do not give
   X the net payoff 2 of Y. The stated outcome can be right despite a wrong payoff.
3) A2.2c: p_R rises, nominal I remains 1200; it is the constraint in
   quantities/purchasing power that tightens. Saying "income falls" would be wrong.
4) A2.2b: (20,4) has utility 80 but costs 1280; the budget comes first, before
   ranking the AFFORDABLE bundles. The last bundle repeats (15,5).
5) A2 elasticities: the proportional % calculation and the exact solution of a
   constant-elasticity function diverge for large changes.
6) E2024 Q4c/d: cost not specified; state the assumption before the figures.
7) E2022 Q4d: bundling costs not specified; the revenues 300/260 are only
   profits at zero cost; explain the assumption.
8) E2025 Q4e: a common shock alone does not demonstrate private signals or
   a winner's curse; specify information and conditional selection.
9) E2020 Q4d: do not invent an incentive conflict before computing
   both expectations. The numbers given align the A/B choice.
10) E2022 taxed monopoly: the tax of 4 is not a real social cost of 4; total DWL 49
    against the efficient no-tax benchmark, unlike the truncated triangle 25.
11) E2025 steel: Q in thousands of tonnes; DWL 10 000 in the diagram
    means 10 million dollars, not 10 000 dollars.
12) E2025 penalty: 60 is enough for weak IC, strictly more than 60 for
    a strict choice of high quality without a tie-breaking rule.

20.2 General errors to spot in every exercise
- Confusing direct demand Q(p) and inverse demand p(Q), or their derivatives.
- Reading the monopoly price on MR instead of on demand.
- Forgetting the term Q*p'(Q) in MR, or replacing MR with p under monopoly.
- Using MR=MC without checking for a maximum, non-negativity, capacity and shutdown.
- With constant MC, a given price above MC and unlimited capacity,
  no finite optimum q exists without another limit; p=MC does not solve
  a maximisation with a constant positive margin.
- Confusing MU,MP,MR,MC,MB or MRS,MRTS,MRT.
- Confusing slope and elasticity; forgetting the factor p/Q and the sign.
- Adding the prices of private demands instead of the quantities.
- Adding public-good demands horizontally.
- Leaving negative demands/quantities in a market sum.
- Deriving firm i's profits with market Q instead of q_i.
- Varying the fixed cost in a marginal derivative where it is constant.
- Shutting down every firm with pi<0 even if R covers VC and FC is unavoidable.
- Claiming "zero economic profit ->no income".
- Equating diminishing marginal product with decreasing returns to scale.
- Confusing PS with profit when FC is non-zero.
- Counting tax revenue as a social loss, or forgetting the budget cost
  of a subsidy; omitting external damages/benefits.
- Measuring DWL against the private Q when an externality already exists.
- Choosing a Pigouvian tax=MEC(Qinitial) when MEC depends on Q.
- Concluding any discount is profitable without considering capacity or arbitrage.
- Applying p_usage=MC to every subscription with heterogeneous customers.
- Confusing a subscription alone with a two-part tariff.
- Forgetting the follower's reaction in the leader's derivative.
- Confusing price competition with homogeneous and with differentiated products.
- Claiming a barrier to entry only affects the short run.
- Confusing adverse selection(type) and moral hazard(action).
- Checking the bank's profitability with pH without checking that the agent chooses H.
- Forgetting the outside option/participation even if IC is satisfied.
- Computing comparative advantage by looking only at absolute productivity.
- Turning an aggregate welfare conclusion into a conclusion for everyone.
- Giving numbers without explaining the mechanism asked for by "explain".

  things equal.
Luxury good: A good for which demand rises more than proportionally with income.
Perfect substitutes: Goods a person is willing to exchange at a constant rate.
Perfect complements: Goods that are useful together in fixed proportions.
Indifference curve: Combinations of goods that give a consumer the same satisfaction.
Engel curve: The quantity a consumer chooses at different incomes, holding prices fixed.
Marginal utility: The extra satisfaction from consuming an additional unit of a good.
Marginal rate of substitution: The amount of one good a consumer is willing
  to give up for a little more of another while remaining equally well off.
Marginal product of capital: The extra output from additional capital,
  holding other inputs fixed.
Decreasing marginal product of capital: Additional capital adds less and
  less output as capital increases while other inputs stay fixed.
Isoquant: Combinations of inputs that produce the same amount of output.
Isocost: Combinations of inputs that have the same total cost.
Marginal rate of technical substitution: The amount of one input that can
  be saved by using more of another while keeping output unchanged.
Increasing returns to scale: Increasing all inputs by a given proportion
  increases output by a greater proportion.
Constant returns to scale: Increasing all inputs by a given proportion
  increases output by the same proportion.
Decreasing returns to scale: Increasing all inputs by a given proportion
  increases output by a smaller proportion.
Economies of scale: Average production cost falls as output increases.
Unit elastic demand: A one-percent price increase reduces quantity demanded
  by one percent, locally and holding other determinants fixed.
Income elasticity: The percentage change in demand associated with a
  one-percent income increase, holding other determinants fixed.
Cross-price elasticity: The percentage change in demand for one good
  associated with a one-percent change in another good's price.
Teaching formulations prepared here; do not present them as
sentences taken from an official marking scheme.

20.5 Answer template for a calculation question
"The firm chooses [variable] to maximize [objective], taking [given]
as given. Its profit is [function]. The first-order condition is [equation],
which gives [quantity/price]. Substituting into [demand/cost] gives [rest].
The result is valid because [domain/maximum/participation]. Intuitively,
[marginal trade-off]. Compared with [benchmark], [change and reason]."
For welfare: state the benchmark, write W with government/externality, compute
the components, draw and explain which transaction is lost/excessive.
For theory: simple definition ->source of the incentive ->consequence
->condition/limit linked to the question.

20.7 Exercise generators to ask ChatGPT for
- Change supply and demand intercepts/slopes then add a tax/subsidy;
  require reading both prices, the public budget and welfare.
- Change U, prices, income; first offer an interior case, then a corner,
  then perfect complements; compare affordable/preferred.
- Give a multi-price demand and ask for own/cross/income elasticities.
- Give a production function; ask for MP, MRTS, returns,
  then minimum cost for an imposed quantity.
- Give TC and a price; ask for q, profit, PS, SR shutdown/LR exit.
- Go monopoly->Cournot->Stackelberg->Bertrand; keep the same
  primitives and ask what changes in the strategic variable.
- Give two markets with different exclusion prices; require comparing
  ALL segments and boundaries under a common price.
- Compare subscription alone, a single price and a two-part tariff.
- Add a variable externality and a badly calibrated tax; ask for the social
  benchmark, not just the direction of the change in quantity.
- Give a public good where one WTP reaches 0 before the candidate optimum;
  force a piecewise vertical summation.
- Change pH,pL, repayment, private benefit and own funds in a credit contract.
- Give monetary matrices and social utilities; recompute best responses.
- Give a PPF and a world price then a tariff; check the prohibitive threshold and units.
- Give a sale auction then a procurement one; explain why the second price
  does not point the same way in both cases.
- Ask for an English explanation without formulas, then a full derivation.

20.8 Final check of an exam answer
Does the model match the words "price/quantity", "public/private",
"fixed fee", "observable", "chooses first"?
Is the variable differentiated the one actually chosen?
Is each quantity non-negative and in the right segment?
Does the FOC describe a valid maximum/minimum, and were corners checked?
Did I use the costs and probabilities belonging to the right agent?
Did I explain the mechanism and not just write down a formula?
Were transfers distinguished from real costs and external damage?
Is the DWL benchmark explicitly SOCIAL and consistent with the question?
Do the graph's axes, areas and units match the calculations?
Is an extra assumption needed, and has it been stated?
